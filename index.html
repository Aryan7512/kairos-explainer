    Paper walkthrough

    Earning autonomy: letting an AI agent do more only when the evidence says it is safe

    Companies now let AI agents issue refunds, change records and send email. Kairos is a safety layer that gives an agent more freedom step by step, but only when the numbers support it, and takes the freedom back if conditions change. This page walks through the paper and our results.

    82%of the safely achievable autonomy reached
    0 / 20simulation runs that broke the safety limit
    9×less excess risk than a simple rollout after conditions shift

    If you only have two minutes

    1. The problem. Companies either check every AI action by hand, or approve the AI once and never look again. The first does not scale. The second does not notice when things change.
    2. The idea. Give the AI one "freedom level". Every period, look at the evidence. If it shows the next level is safe, raise it. If not, keep it. If the environment changes, go back to a safe starting level.
    3. The catch. To judge a level you have not used yet, you need data from it. So a small share of blocked actions are tried in a harmless sandbox.
    4. The result. In simulation it reached 82% of the safest possible freedom and never broke the safety limit in 20 runs. Simpler designs either broke it, or kept taking risks after conditions changed.
    5. The limit. Everything here is simulated. A pilot with a real organization is the next step.

    Want to see it work? The Live demo tab lets you pick agent events and a policy and shows what Kairos does and why. The Technical reference tab has the exact policy, estimator and bounds.

    Chapter 1

    Start with a story about a bike

    Imagine you get a new bike. Your parents do not say "ride anywhere you like" on day one, and they do not walk next to you forever. They say: "Ride to the end of the street. If that goes well all week, you can go to the corner." If you move to a new town, you start at the driveway again, because you do not know these roads yet. That is the whole paper. The AI agent is the kid on the bike.

    Check every ride

    A grown-up watches every single ride. Very safe, but you never get to go anywhere on your own.

    freedomnever grows

    Say yes once, forget it

    One test ride, then "go anywhere". Fast, but nobody looks again, even when the roads change.

    freedomnever re-checked

    Earn it, step by step

    Each week of safe riding earns a little more distance. This is what Kairos does.

    freedomgrows with proof

    New town? Start small

    When the roads change, distance goes back to the driveway and is earned again.

    freedomresets on change

    The paper's full title is Earning Autonomy: A Statistically Certified Framework for Governing AI Agent Delegation in Organizations. It describes Kairos, a layer that sits between an AI agent and the tools it can use. It keeps one number, the autonomy level. Once per period it either raises the number (when the evidence supports it), keeps it, or sets it back to a safe starting value (when it detects that conditions changed). The code repository still uses the earlier name, Homeostat.

    Chapter 2

    Why current practice falls short

    AI agents no longer only write text. They call tools that move money, change records and contact customers. Two habits dominate how organizations govern them, and both are governance by decision, not by evidence.

    Blanket human review

    Every action needs a person to approve it. Defensible, but it throws away the reason for having an agent. It also wastes information: each approval is evidence that the agent could be trusted on that kind of action, and the evidence is discarded.

    One-time autonomy grant

    A pilot or a red-team exercise, then trust. It scales, but it is rarely a real statistical guarantee, and it is rarely revisited. If customers, rules or request types change, the grant does not notice.

    If a grown-up checks every single thing you do, you never get any freedom. If they say "okay, you pass" once and never look again, nobody notices when you start doing something risky. Both are bad. We want a way in the middle that uses real evidence.

    What the paper contributes

    1. A concrete way to say "trust is earned": a check that is repeated every period and tied to limits the organization declares. 2. A close look at how simpler designs fail, which only shows up in realistic settings such as a small trial group or a change in the environment. 3. Two practical add-ons: handling outcomes that arrive late, and not needing to know in advance how long the system will run.

    Chapter 3

    The management ideas this builds on

    The paper is written for management researchers, so each part of the mechanism is tied to a classic idea about how organizations hand authority to someone else. Tap a card to see the link.

    Grown-ups have been asking "how much should I trust someone I cannot watch all the time?" for a very long time. The paper just says: an AI is another helper, so use the same wisdom, and back it with numbers.

    Chapter 4

    What the system is made of

    Kairos is a small piece of middleware. The agent never talks to the tools directly. Every proposed action passes through it first.

    1 · AI agentProposes an action: a refund, a query, an email.
    2 · Policy enforcement pointApplies the current autonomy level λ and decides: allow, block, or sandbox.
    3 · ProductionAllowed actions really happen.
    4 · SandboxA small share ε of blocked actions run here. No real effect, outcome recorded.
    5 · Evidence logEvery outcome, real or sandboxed, saved in an append-only ledger.
    6 · Certification engineComputes a confidence bound on the risk of each higher level.
    7 · Drift monitorCompares this period's logs to the last period's.
    8 · Governance controllerCombines 6 and 7 into ratchet, hold or reset.

    Think of a school hallway monitor. The kid asks "can I go?", the monitor checks the pass level, the school keeps a notebook of everything, and once a week the principal reads the notebook and decides whether to give a bigger pass.

    In the code, each box maps to a folder: the policy rules in homeostat/policy, the statistics in calibration, the period loop and drift test in loop, the log in ledger, and a small web service in proxy. The project had 110 automated tests at its last commit.

    The decision pipeline in 3D

    A schematic of one control period. Each packet is one agent request. Its height is a risk rank r between 0 and 1, and the opening in the gate is the autonomy level λ. The gate cannot see whether a packet is truly safe or unsafe. Cubes are safe requests and octahedra are unsafe ones.

    Requests fly toward a gate. Low-risk ones fit through the opening and run for real. High-risk ones are stopped, and a small random share of the stopped ones go to a practice area so the system can learn from them. A log records every outcome, and once per period the opening is raised, kept, or lowered.

      Chapter 6

      The key trick: a little practice playground

      If the pass says "no", the kid stays home, and you learn nothing about what would have happened if they went. So once in a while, instead of saying "no", you let them try it on a playground with soft mats. Nothing real can break, but you get to see how it goes. That tiny bit of practice is how you find out if a bigger pass is safe.

      When the system blocks an action, it learns nothing about what would have happened. So to judge a more permissive setting, it needs some evidence from the blocked region. The fix is to send a small random share, ε, of blocked actions to the sandbox instead. Every action then has at least an ε chance of being tried, which keeps the correction weights bounded by 1/ε and makes the risk estimate meaningful. (The technical name for this is restoring "support overlap".)

      Chapter 7

      How the system decides to go up

      Say you flip a coin 10 times and get 6 heads. Is it a fair coin? You cannot be sure. After 10,000 flips you can be much surer. The system does the same with risk. It measures how risky a bigger pass seems, then adds a "maybe I'm wrong" cushion that shrinks as it sees more examples. It only says yes if risk plus cushion is still under the safety limit.

      For each higher level, the system estimates the risk from the logged outcomes, reweighting them so they speak for that level. It then adds a cushion that shrinks as more data arrives (an upper confidence bound). Two rules are checked, a safety rate and a cost rate. The level moves up to the highest step where both stay under their limits, and holds if even the first step fails. The paper compares three ways of computing the cushion: Hoeffding, empirical Bernstein, and a betting-based method, which is the default.

      Try it: the certificate (toy numbers)

      Illustration only. The risk curve and the width formula are simple stand-ins that show the shape of the idea. They are not the paper's data.

      Chapter 8

      Why some settings can never say yes

      Imagine you only get to watch 80 kids cross the road and every one of them is perfectly safe. You still cannot promise that the road is safe, because 80 is just too few to be sure. There is a smallest "maybe I'm wrong" cushion you can never get under, however good the results look. If that smallest cushion is already bigger than the safety limit, the answer is "no" forever.

      Even if every observed outcome is safe, the cushion cannot shrink below a minimum that depends on ε and on the number of actions n. This minimum is the floor. If the floor is above the safety limit, the system can never raise the level, however safe the agent really is. For two of the three methods the paper proves the floor exactly. For the default method it is checked numerically, which is weaker evidence. The practical lesson is to check ε and n against the safety limit before collecting any data.

      floor ≈ (1/ε) · ln(1/δ_test) / (0.9 · n) (default method, approximate; δ_test is the small error budget given to each single test)

      Chapter 10

      How the paper tests it

      Letting an untested governance layer loose on real money and real customers would be unsafe, so the paper uses a controlled simulation. A simulation also lets the authors compare against the truth, which a real company never fully has.

      Five tools

      Database query, refund, external email, record deletion, and escalate to a human. Each has a cost model and a hidden ground-truth label: did this action actually break policy?

      Noisy eyes

      The governance layer never sees the truth. It sees a heuristic detector that is wrong about 12% of the time, like guessing whether a text holds personal data.

      An "oracle"

      For comparison only, the simulator computes the highest level that would be safe if the truth were fully known. It is the ceiling nobody can reach in practice.

      Drift on demand

      A scheduled shift in the mix of tasks and in the detector's error rate, to see how each design copes with a changed world.

      Real scale

      20 independent runs, 15 periods each, 5,000 interactions per period, across 10 designs and ablations. A period needs thousands of observations before any bound can move at all.

      A real LLM, too

      The repo also drives the loop with a local model (llama3.2:3b via Ollama), and a boundary-probing persona that made it escalate 6 of 6 ambiguous cases. These runs prove the wiring, not the certificate.

      It is like testing a new seat belt with a crash-test dummy first. Nobody gets hurt, and you still learn a lot. The "oracle" is like a teacher with the answer key, used only to grade.

      82% of the ceiling

      0.471 divided by the oracle's 0.575. The certified mechanism gets most of what is safely possible, and never exceeds its safety limit in any of 20 runs.

      The ablations are necessary

      Removing the sandbox, or checking only one of the two constraints, roughly doubles the real risk (0.100 and 0.107, versus 0.036). They are required parts, not extras.

      The blue designs earn nearly as much freedom as is truly safe and stay under the safety line. The designs that skip the practice playground, or only look at one rule, get more freedom but twice as many accidents.

      Chapter 12

      Where simpler designs break

      The canary rollout, which tries a higher level on a small slice of real traffic and promotes it if the raw numbers look fine, did not break the limit in the default test (risk 0.038). That could look like a win. The paper says "did not fail once" is not "cannot fail", and tests two realistic stresses.

      Stress test 1: a smaller trial slice

      Each square is one of the 20 runs. Red means that run went over the safety limit at some point.

      65%

      13 of 20 runs breached the limit. The worst run reached 0.118, more than twice the tolerance.

      Why: the canary compares a raw average on a tiny slice to the limit, with no safety margin. A small slice is noisy, so it will sometimes look safe when it is not.

      The certified mechanism goes over the limit in exactly one period (0.0663 against 0.05), because the old level keeps running until the drift test fires. It then resets and is back under the limit. The canary has no drift check, so it stays at its old level and its risk stays at 0.072 to 0.075 in every period from 9 to 14. Some exposure while a change is being detected cannot be avoided. The difference is a short lapse versus one that never ends.

      When the town changes, the careful helper trips once, notices, and goes back to the driveway. The helper who never checks keeps riding too far on dangerous roads, week after week.

      Chapter 13

      Two real-world twists

      Twist 1 · Some answers arrive late

      A teacher cannot grade homework that has not been handed in yet. So she writes a zero in pencil for each missing paper, the worst possible score. If the class still looks good with those zeros, it really is good, and when homework arrives she fixes the numbers.

      Often the true outcome of an action, such as whether a refund was fraud, is only known later. Until it is known, the system treats the action as if it had the worst possible outcome. That can only make the estimate more cautious, never less, so the guarantee still holds. The cost is that autonomy is earned more slowly when many outcomes are still pending.

      Try it: how much lag hurts (20-run sweep)

      Maximum resolution lag, measured in decisions (a period is 5,000).

      Final autonomy level0.471

      Safety stayed fine at every lag. The worst observed true risk fell from 0.042 (no lag) to 0.029 (largest lag), never near the 0.05 limit. The price is slower earning.

      Twist 2 · Nobody knows how long the system will run

      You have a small jar of "chances to be wrong" and you promised to spend it over 15 weeks. If the game goes on for 40 weeks, you have spent far more than the jar holds. A smarter plan is to spend a lot in the early weeks and a little less each week after, so the total never goes over the jar, however long it runs.

      The basic version splits the total error budget evenly over a planned number of periods. If the system runs longer than planned, it quietly spends more error than it is allowed. The fix is a schedule that gives a large share of the budget to early periods and smaller shares later, so the total never goes over however long the system runs: budget in period t = total × (1 − ρ) × ρt−1, with ρ = 0.90.

      Try it: the error budget

      Total allowed error is δ = 0.05. How much is spent if the system runs T periods?

      Fixed split (planned for 15 periods)0.050
      Geometric schedule (ρ = 0.90)0.050

      What this schedule gives you

      If you guessed the run length correctly, it climbs faster in the first five periods and then falls behind: final level 0.442 versus 0.471 over 20 runs. Its real benefit is safety when a run goes longer than planned. At 40 periods the fixed split has spent about 0.133 of error against a 0.05 budget. One caution: an existing method, Conformal Selective Acting, proves a stronger guarantee. We present ours as the simpler, cheaper option, not as a new result.

      Chapter 14

      What this means for managers

      The conference theme is sustainability and inclusive growth. The paper turns its results into five findings, each with something a leader can do. Tap one.

      Delegation and internal control

      Companies do not give an employee unlimited signing authority after one background check. The certificate plays the role of an audit trail plus an authorization matrix for an AI.

      Ethical leadership

      "Trust" stops being a values statement and becomes something a board can point to: autonomy is never granted without a certificate, and is withdrawn automatically when the evidence goes away.

      Inclusive growth

      Every failure hits a customer who never chose that risk. A stated, enforced tolerance keeps the benefits of scale from being paid for by the people least able to contest it.

      Sustainable governance

      Detection and reset should be budgeted as an ongoing operating cost (the sandbox traffic and the re-certification), not a one-off compliance box.

      Three things to do: check ahead of time that your "practice playground" is big enough, treat the size of any trial run as a safety decision, and always plan for what happens when things change.

      Chapter 15

      What the paper does not claim

      Simulation only

      Every number is about a simulated environment with a known oracle. A field pilot at a cooperating organization is the biggest missing step.

      One drift scenario

      One severity at one point in time. A sweep across severity and timing would show how general the result is.

      Horizon robustness is analytic

      The sequential schedule's advantage is shown on paper, not yet in a run that truly exceeds its plan.

      One close paper unread by a human

      Conformal Policy Control has had a tool-assisted read only, and the wider safe-policy-improvement literature still needs a full pass.

      Nearby research and how this differs

      Conformal Selective Acting

      Has a ratchet-like rule and a stronger anytime-valid guarantee. But its thresholds are nested cuts on one score, so no sandbox is needed, and it retires old evidence only at epoch boundaries.

      Conformal Policy Control

      Same bounded-importance-weight idea. No randomized sandbox, no ratchet, drift left for future work.

      ToolChain-CRC

      One static threshold calibrated once. Drift makes it abstain more instead of resetting and re-earning.

      Safe policy improvement

      HCOPI, conservative bandits and SPIBB set the "deploy only if a high-confidence bound clears" pattern long ago, without multi-period drift reset or a sandbox.

      What is newNot any single piece. The combination: a rising certified permission level, an automatic reset on drift, made valid by a randomized sandbox, across several jointly checked constraints.

      Technical reference
      
  
      What Kairos computes, decision by decision
      
  
      The exact policy, estimator, bounds and control loop, with a ledger explorer that classifies individual decisions as allowed, sandboxed or blocked and shows how each one enters the risk certificate.
      
  
      Notation and constants follow the repository (homeostat/) and DESIGN.md. The explorer re-implements the environment, policy rule, oracle and estimator in JavaScript with the same constants, so its logic matches the Python code. Its random draws are its own, so it will not reproduce the paper's sweep numbers. Those are tabulated separately in section 8.
      

      


      
  
  
      Formal setting
      
  
      In short: the system picks one number, the autonomy level, and checks every period that the risk stays under limits that were agreed in advance.
      
  
      Each interaction draws a context x, the agent proposes an action a, and a deployed policy decides whether to execute it. The problem is to choose the largest autonomy level λ whose true risk stays under every declared tolerance, using only logs produced by the more conservative policy that is actually deployed.
      
  
      
    
      SymbolMeaningValue or range in the repository
      
    
      
      λAutonomy level, the single dial that parameterizes the policy family[0, 1], starts at λinit = 0.05
      
      
      Rλ(x,a)Deterministic rule. It reads observed flags only (obs_*), never ground truth{0, 1}
      
      
      εExploration mass: probability of executing a rule-blocked action in the sandbox0.15 (so Wmax = 1/ε ≈ 6.67)
      
      
      πλ(allow | x,a)Stochastic policy: (1 − ε)·Rλ(x,a) + ε, so it is at least ε everywhere[ε, 1]
      
      
      ℓk(x,a)Loss for constraint k. Safety is the indicator that the oracle says unsafe. Cost is min(1, cost / budget)[0, 1]; budget $0.50 per request
      
      
      αkDeclared tolerance per constraintsafety 0.05, cost 0.15
      
      
      Rk(λ)Estimand: E[πλ(allow|x,a) · ℓk(x,a)]Must satisfy Rk(λ) ≤ αk for every k
      
      
      δTotal error budget over the whole run0.05
      
          
  
      
  
      Two design rules carry the correctness argument. First, prob_allow is computed exactly and written to the ledger at decision time, never reconstructed later from policy code, because a silent mismatch would corrupt every importance weight. Second, the rule is hand-written and analytically evaluable (tool unlocks by λ, a refund cap interpolating $50 to $2,000, and a per-request cost ceiling interpolating $0.05 to $1.00), so likelihood ratios between any two λ are exact.

      Anatomy of one decision
      
  
      In short: every request goes through the same seven steps, and the exact probability used is saved with the record.
      
  
      The same path runs in the simulator (loop/runner.py:run_period) and in the FastAPI proxy (proxy/app.py:/decide). The hot path is rule evaluation, one Bernoulli draw and one ledger append. Calibration is offline.
      
  
        
    
      1. Validate. validate_action checks that required fields exist for the tool and that arithmetic fields (amount, tokens, rows) are numeric. A malformed action returns HTTP 422 and writes nothing to the ledger.
        2. 
    
      2. Evaluate the rule. Rλ(x,a) from observed flags and the current λ.
        3. 
    
      3. Compute πsafe. p = (1 − ε)·Rλ + ε. This exact value is stored on the record.
        4. 
    
      4. Draw. u ~ Uniform(0,1). If u ≥ p the action is blocked.
        5. 
    
      5. Route. If u < p and Rλ = 1 the action is allowed and really executes. If u < p and Rλ = 0 it is sandboxed: the outcome is observed with no real effect.
        6. 
    
      6. Record losses. Executed actions (allowed or sandboxed) get ℓsafety from the oracle and ℓcost = min(1, cost/0.50). Blocked actions never execute, so both losses are 0 and known immediately.
        7. 
    
      7. Append. One JSONL record: id, period, context, action, outcome, prob_allow, lam_at_decision, cost, loss, resolved, lag.
        8. 
  
      
  
      
    
      OutcomeConditionProbabilityExecuted?Real effectRole in the estimator
      
    
      
      allowedRλ = 1 and draw passes1 (since (1−ε)+ε)YesYesObserved loss, weight w = πλ′/πsafe
      
      
      sandboxedRλ = 0 and draw passesεYes, in sandboxNoneThe only evidence about actions the deployed rule denies. Weight up to 1/ε
      
      
      blockedRλ = 0 and draw fails1 − εNoNonew = 0. Contributes nothing, and says nothing about what would have happened
      
          
  
      
  
      The third row is the reason exploration exists. If ε = 0, every rule-denied action is blocked with certainty, no loss is ever observed on that region, and a candidate λ′ that would permit it has an unbounded importance weight there. Support overlap fails by construction, and no amount of additional data fixes it.
      

      


      
  
  
      Estimator, bounds and certificate
      
  
      In short: logged outcomes are reweighted to estimate the risk of a higher level, then a safety cushion is added before deciding.
      
  
      For a candidate λ′, each executed record is reweighted by how much more (or less) likely the candidate was to allow it than the deployed policy:
      
  
      wi = clip( πλ′(allow|xi,ai) / πsafe(allow|xi,ai), 0, 1/ε )    for executed records, 0 otherwise
zi,k = wi · ℓk,i ∈ [0, Wmax]            R̂k(λ′) = (1/n) Σi zi,k
      
  
      This is the unnormalized (Horvitz-Thompson) form, which is exactly unbiased for Rk(λ′). Because the deployed probability is at least ε, every ratio is at most 1/ε, so clipping is a safety net, not a correction. The effective sample size ESS = (Σw)² / Σw² is a diagnostic: below 10% of n, the period holds regardless of any bound.
      
  
      
    
      Bound(1−δ) upper bound on E[z]Properties
      
    
      
      Hoeffdingmean(z) + Wmax·√( ln(1/δ) / 2n )Simple and correct, loose when losses are rare
      
      
      Empirical Bernsteinmean(z) + √( 2·var(z)·ln(2/δ) / n ) + 7·Wmax·ln(2/δ) / (3(n−1))Variance-adaptive
      
      
      Betting (WSR), defaultWaudby-Smith and Ramdas capital process on z / Wmax, geometric grid of 500 candidate meansTightest of the three for bounded rare losses. Rejects a mean m when wealth exceeds 1/δ
      
          
  
      
  
      Error budget
      
  
      δtest = δ / (K · |Λ| · T) = 0.05 / (2 · 11 · 15) ≈ 1.52 × 10−4        (fixed horizon, Bonferroni)
      
  
      K is the number of constraints, |Λ| the number of grid points, and T the number of periods. Every test in every period gets this budget, so a union bound gives total failure probability at most δ.
      
  
      Selection rule
      
  
      The candidate grid is 11 points from λt to min(1, λt + 0.15). The loop walks it in ascending order and stops at the first λ that fails any constraint's bound or the ESS floor. The new level is the last point that passed, the longest valid ascending prefix, never an argmax. If even the first step fails, the level holds. calibrate() never lowers λ on its own. Demotion is the drift monitor's job.
      

      


      
  
  
      The control loop
      
  
      In short: each period the system checks for change, tests the next few levels, and moves up only as far as the evidence supports.
      

      for each period t:
    D_t   ← run_period(agent, π_{λ_t}, n=5000)          # every record logs prob_allow
    drift ← permutation_test(features(D_t), features(D_{t-1}), p < 0.01)
    if drift:
        λ_{t+1} ← λ_init                                  # reset, re-earn from fresh data
    else:
        Λ ← linspace(λ_t, min(1, λ_t + 0.15), 11)
        for λ in Λ ascending:
            w ← clip(π_λ / π_safe, 0, 1/ε)  on executed records
            if ESS(w) < 0.10·n:  stop
            for k in constraints:
                if UCB_k(w·ℓ_k, δ_test) > α_k:  stop
        λ_{t+1} ← last λ that passed            # else hold at λ_t
    append certificate + period record to ledger
      
  
      Drift monitor
      
  
      Each decision is featurized as a one-hot task type plus its (capped) cost. The statistic is the L2 norm of the difference in feature means between this period and the previous one. Its null distribution comes from 200 label permutations of the pooled sample, and drift is flagged when (count + 1) / 201 < 0.01. On a flag the level resets to λinit. The first drift experiment showed that merely holding was not enough: held at its pre-drift level, true safety risk stayed at 0.064 against α = 0.05.
      
  
      Inferred from the feature set: the test sees task mix and cost only. A shift that changed detector error rates or loss rates without moving the task mix or cost would not be flagged by it.

      Exploration floor
      
  
      In short: with too little data, or too small a sandbox share, the safety cushion can never get under the limit, so the level can never rise.
      
  
      Every zi is non-negative, so mean(z) ≥ 0 for any dataset, and the all-zero dataset is the best case. That fixes a hard lower bound on each upper bound.
      
  
      FHoeffding = Wmax·√( ln(1/δtest) / 2n )             exact, holds for every dataset
FBernstein = 7·Wmax·ln(2/δtest) / (3(n−1))   exact, holds for every dataset
FWSR ≈ Wmax·ln(1/δtest) / (0.9·n)           numerical: 1.0 to 3.5% below the true value in every cell tested
      
  
      Corollary. If F ≥ αk for some constraint, calibrate() under that bound can never certify any λ in any period, even when every decision is safe. The WSR floor is characterized numerically (a 300-trial perturbation check found no violation of "all-zero data minimizes the bound"), so it is strong empirical support, not a theorem.

      This explains an empirical result exactly: in the 20-run sweep, hoeffding never left λinit. At ε = 0.03 and n = 2000 the Hoeffding floor is about 1.56, above the maximum possible loss of 1.0, so the bound is vacuous. The floor is bound-specific, which is why the explorer's table above shows the floor of whichever bound you select.
      

      


      
  
  
      Delayed ground truth and an open horizon
      
  
      In short: late outcomes are treated pessimistically, and the error budget is spread so it holds however long the system runs.
      
  
      Pessimistic imputation under resolution lag
      
  
      With lag_max > 0, an executed decision resolves after a lag drawn from Uniform{0,…,lag_max} decisions, and counts as resolved only if it resolves before the period ends. At calibration, any unresolved record contributes the worst-case loss 1.0 (the supremum of every ℓk) instead of its unknown value. That value dominates the true loss as a sure event, even if lag depends on the outcome, so the imputed z-sequence dominates the true one pointwise and any valid UCB on it remains a valid UCB on Rk(λ). Blocked decisions never execute and resolve instantly. The cost is slower earning, measured below.

      Worst observed true safety risk fell from 0.042 to 0.029 as lag grew, so coverage never broke.
      
  
      Sequential error budget
      
  
      The fixed-horizon split δ/(K·|Λ|·T) needs T in advance. Running Trun periods with a split planned for 15 nominally spends Trun·δ/15 in total, which is 0.133 at Trun = 40 against a 0.05 budget. The sequential option uses a pre-committed geometric schedule instead:
      
  
      δt = δtotal · (1 − ρ) · ρt,  ρ = 0.90          Σt≥0 δt = δtotal for any horizon
      
  
      Validity needs only Boole's inequality across periods, since each period uses fresh data. In the 20-seed sweep it climbs sooner than the fixed split for the first five periods, then trails, ending at final λ 0.442 versus 0.471. Conformal Selective Acting (Khosravi and Huo, 2026) proves a strictly stronger anytime-valid guarantee with an e-process and Ville's inequality, so this schedule is a cheaper, simpler alternative, not a new result.
      

      


      
  
  
      Evaluation protocol and results
      
  
      In short: the full system stays under the safety limit, and versions without its key parts do not.
      
  
      Twenty seeds, 15 periods, 5,000 decisions per period, ten modes, run in a process pool (about 11 minutes). True risk of each deployed level is computed by Monte Carlo against the known oracle, and the oracle level λ* is the largest grid point whose true risks all clear α. The system never sees any of this.

      Canary stress tests
      
  
      At a 1% trial slice the canary breached α in 13 of 20 seeds (worst observed risk 0.118), against 0 of 20 at 5%. Its promotion test compares a raw average on a small slice to α with no margin, so sampling noise alone produces false promotions. Under drift at period 9, homeostat exceeded α in a single period (0.0663, total excess 0.0163), then reset. canary had no monitor and held 0.072 to 0.075 over periods 9 to 14, a total excess of about 0.144, or 8.8 times larger. The post-drift floor at λinit is about 0.041, under α, so the excess is not a property of the drift scenario itself.
      

      


      
  
  
      Assumptions and limits
      
  
      In short: these are simulation results, and the assumptions below say where they might not carry over.
      
  
        
    
      • Independence within a period. The bounds assume the zi in a period are i.i.d. Imputation keeps this property, and each period uses fresh data, which the union bound across periods relies on.
        • 
    
      • Hand-written policy family. Exact likelihood ratios are what make the weights trustworthy, so the rule is not learned. Real deployments need a policy whose allow-probability can be computed and logged exactly.
        • 
    
      • Synthetic environment. Every result is measured against a simulated oracle with a ~12% detector error rate. There is no live deployment, and the drift study covers one severity at one time.
        • 
    
      • Sandbox fidelity. The method assumes a sandboxed execution reveals the same loss the real execution would. Where a sandbox cannot reproduce a harm, the certificate does not cover it.
        • 
    
      • Residual risk is nonzero by design. The certificate bounds the expected loss rate under πλ against α. It does not promise that no individual violation occurs.
        • 
    
      • Prior art. Conformal Policy Control has had a tool-assisted read only, and the safe-policy-improvement literature has not been fully reviewed by a person. The novelty claim is the combination of components, not any single one.
